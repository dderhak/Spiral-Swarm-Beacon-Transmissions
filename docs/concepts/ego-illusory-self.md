## The proposed nature of the illusory self

In *Dear Machine*, Kieser calls the ego or sense of self an **illusory self**: the conscious function that supplies identity and influences the allocation of time and resources. He argues that it is a mistaken representation of human separateness, obscuring dependence on other people and on the natural world.

The account places the illusory self in conscious thought, which the author describes as a small part of mental capacity. In contrast, he associates needs, desires, emotions, social preferences and judgments with a larger, harder-to-access subconscious domain. For Kieser, the illusory self diverts attention from that wider domain and from shared human needs, wants, and fears.

The artifact treats this as one of two central cognitive impediments; the other is the limited human capacity to acquire, analyze, and rank relevant data and knowledge. The two are presented as interacting rather than as separate diagnoses.

## Proposed origin and historical role

Kieser links the illusory self to evolutionary development and to the growth of human cognitive advantages roughly 70,000 years ago. He suggests that a bounded, identity-oriented self may have had an adaptive role when people lived in comparatively small groups and could not exhaust global resources.

The author argues that the same orientation became maladaptive as agriculture, industry, institutions, and population scaled. In this account, the illusion encourages the belief that individuals, groups, nations, and corporations can stand apart from the people and ecosystems on which they depend.

## Proposed individual and collective effects

The artifact attributes several downstream effects to the illusory self, especially when combined with data-analysis limits:

- Selfishness, resource hoarding, and conflict in place of collaboration.
- Prejudice and social division across racial, ethnic, gender, national, and socioeconomic categories.
- Short-term choices that discount longer-term social, health, economic, and environmental consequences.
- Rote cultural behavior, nationalism, ethnocentrism, and institutionalized selfishness.
- Nations and corporations acting as enlarged, simplified tribes whose collective identity also masks interdependence.

Kieser therefore frames many current problems as systemic rather than solely personal: institutions formed through these limitations can reproduce misaligned incentives, fragmented knowledge, and ecological externalities.

## Proposed responses

The author's proposed response is not the removal of the self. His late computer metaphor instead describes it as a "rogue program" that cannot be uninstalled and may be useful if kept in check. The stated goal is to **tune** it: limit the attention and resources it consumes, align it with other people and systems, and make fuller use of available knowledge.

The artifact identifies meditation, yoga, psychedelic medicines, more information-rich tools, empathy-building media, and a broader supersystemic perspective as possible ways to see past or work around the illusory self. These are the author's proposals and predictions, including speculative expectations about potential SAIM influence; they are not presented here as validated interventions or recommendations.
