## Definition

The artifact uses **supersystem** for the greater ecosystem of ecosystems on Earth. Its proposed scope includes natural ecosystems, human bodies and communities, economies, governments, technologies, ideas, ideologies, and future SAIM ecosystems.

The term is presented as a practical vocabulary for discussing relationships among systems that are usually studied separately.

## Historical and cognitive context

The artifact frames human history as a sequence of changing relationships with the natural world and with one another: hunter-gatherer, agricultural, industrial, and information eras. It identifies two recurring cognitive impediments:

- an “illusory self” that obscures interdependence;
- limited human ability to acquire, analyze, and prioritize large bodies of data and knowledge.

Within the source’s argument, these limitations become embedded in institutions, incentives, economies, food systems, health systems, and ideologies.

## Diversity and complexity

The artifact proposes diversity and complexity as characteristics associated with healthier, more resilient ecosystems. It applies this claim across forests, soils, microbiomes, economies, schools, communities, and idea systems.

This is the source’s cross-disciplinary hypothesis, not a repository-level scientific conclusion. The artifact itself notes that much of the evidence it cites is preliminary or correlational.

## Seeding and nurturing

The proposed problem-solving strategy is to **seed and nurture** diversity and complexity in degraded or underdeveloped ecosystems rather than relying only on simplification, standardization, or short-term suppression.

Examples discussed in the source include regenerative agriculture, adaptive learning, ecological restoration, microbiome interventions, brain-development programs, blockchains, and new ways of disseminating ideas. These examples remain source claims and should be evaluated individually.

## Supersystemic innovators

The artifact groups apparently unrelated innovators under a shared “supersystemic” pattern: identify degraded ecosystems, increase useful diversity and complexity, and measure long-term effects across system boundaries. The proposed value of the label is to make connections among disciplines visible and retrievable.
